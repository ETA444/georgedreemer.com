My MSc in Marketing Analytics & Data Science at the University of Groningen was a graduate program focused on applied statistics, machine learning, data science, and data engineering.

The program covered the full path from research design and data preparation to modelling, evaluation, and communication: statistical analysis, supervised learning, segmentation, text mining, sentiment analysis, time-series work, and business-facing decision support across consumer and retail contexts.

In practice, I did this work in Python and R for modelling, data wrangling, and automation, and in Stata and SPSS for statistical testing and survey-style analysis. I used SQL for data engineering and extraction, and I turned the results into data visualizations, written reports, and presentations. The code, reports, and slides behind the theses and projects are publicly browsable, with the programming linked through my GitHub.

Data Pre-MSc Thesis

Earnings Call Sentiment & Stock Volatility

  • Asked whether the sentiment of an earnings call shapes how volatile a stock is afterwards, using 178 call transcripts from 10 NASDAQ-listed firms (2016–2020).
  • Cleaned the transcripts and scored them with NLTK's VADER, then scraped stock and NASDAQ index prices for the 10 days either side of each call to measure the change in volatility, and tested sentiment with multiple linear regression.
  • Volatility rose after 87% of calls, yet sentiment was not a statistically significant predictor. The paper reports this plainly and points to other factors worth testing, while showing the call itself is a decisive moment for volatility.

MSc thesis · University of Groningen · 2024 · Grade: 8 / 10

Willingness to Share Health Data in Italy: Investigation under a Multi-context Paradigm with DCE Data

What makes people willing to share their health data beyond its original use? I analysed choices from 329 respondents in Italy to understand how the organisations involved, the intended use, consent arrangements, and external oversight shape that decision.

329 in Italy
Latent preference groups

Analysis tools
R & Stata 17
Research question

How do different data-sharing arrangements influence willingness to allow the secondary use of health data?

Five contextual attributes were examined together:

  • Original data collector
  • New data user
  • Purpose of reuse
  • Information & consent
  • External review
Analytical approach

I analysed discrete-choice-experiment data supplied by Biasiotto and colleagues: 329 participants in Italy.

  1. Compared five models in Stata (three latent class specifications, a conditional logit, and a logistic regression), judging fit by AIC and log-likelihood.
  2. Interpreted a two-class latent class model as the primary analysis, with the conditional logit as a secondary check.
  3. Calculated average relative attribute importance from the primary model.
Finding & relevance

Respondents leaned toward not sharing, but context shifted that.

Two preference groups (46.5% and 53.5%) ranked the five attributes identically:

  1. Who reuses the data
  2. Purpose of reuse
  3. Information about sharing
  4. Review of the transfer
  5. Original data collector

Yet they preferred different options within each attribute. On who reuses the data, their orders were contrasting. One-size-fits-all rules are therefore unlikely to fit, while transparency and some form of external review were common ground.

Inside the experiment

Same health data. Different sharing context.

A willingness-to-share decision involves more than the data itself. Change who collected it, who wants to reuse it, why, and under which safeguards. These were the five contextual attributes examined together in the research.

What the analysis revealed

Two groups agreed on what matters, and disagreed on what they prefer.

Overall, respondents leaned toward not sharing. Willingness depended on the arrangement, and the five attributes did not carry equal weight.

Importance of the five attributes

  1. New data user
  2. Reason for new data use
  3. Information & consent
  4. External review
  5. Original data collector

Ranking based on average relative attribute importance in the primary two-class latent class model. Both modelled groups shared the same attribute-importance order, while differing in their preferred levels within several attributes.

An expectation challenged

I expected information & consent to be the most influential attribute and the reason for reuse to rank second. The second expectation held; the first did not. The new data user ranked first and information & consent third, so who receives the data mattered more than how consent is organised.

What it suggests

Plan for different audiences, not one average citizen.

The two groups' contrasting preferences sit alongside a shared emphasis on transparency and oversight.

Expect heterogeneity inside a country
Within one Italian sample, two groups held contrasting preferences on most attributes. A single average preference would have hidden that.
Be cautious about one-size-fits-all rules
Some results differ from larger multinational studies. That suggests data-reuse policy may not fit every European region equally, and that country-specific consideration and research are worthwhile.
Treat transparency and review as common ground
Being informed and having some form of external review matched earlier research. In both groups, the most preferred information arrangement was an informed one, and the most preferred review option was some form of review. This supports transparency, clarity of purpose and independent oversight as institutional practice.

These are research-informed considerations, not a tested intervention or a demonstrated increase in real-world sharing.

Research decisions and supporting evidence

Sample, data checks, and descriptive analysis

Fieldwork ran from April to August 2021: 929 survey initiations, 362 completions, and 329 respondents after removing completions under five minutes.

Before modelling I checked for missing values and outliers. No variable had more than 3.95% missing values, and none were independent variables. Thirty-four respondents had unusually long completion times (over 30 minutes). I reviewed their written feedback, found nothing abnormal, and discussed the pattern with my supervisor. The survey software let people pause and resume, so I did not treat the durations as a data-quality problem.

The final sample had a mean age of 50.83 years (SD 14.55), was 53% female and 47% male, and was most often educated to high-school level (42.1%) or master's level (about 27%). 54.6% reported no chronic condition, and 32.1% had sufficient eHealth literacy (37.6% problematic, 30.3% inadequate).

Fieldwork, recruitment and the experimental design were carried out by the source research team.

How the choice experiment worked

The source experiment used 32 unique choice tasks, arranged into four blocks of eight. Each participant completed two sets of eight decisions, covering two assigned health-information themes from lifestyle, physical health, mental health, and genetic information.

Each task presented a sharing context. Participants decided whether to accept or reject sharing under those arrangements, rather than selecting a favourite organisation or purpose in isolation.

This structure enabled the analysis to examine contextual trade-offs across the five attributes. The health-information themes are part of the experimental setting; they are not an additional attribute in the five-factor explorer above.

Sixteen decisions per participant are repeated observations from the same people—not sixteen independent respondents.

Model selection and attribute importance

After checking correlations between the independent variables for multicollinearity, I fitted five models of three types in Stata 17: three latent class models (one class; two classes without starting values; two classes with starting values from the one-class model), a conditional logit, and a logistic regression. I compared them by AIC and log-likelihood.

The two best-fitting models were the two-class latent class model with starting values and the conditional logit. I treated the former as the primary analysis and the latter as a secondary check. A latent class model divides respondents into classes by their expressed preferences instead of assuming one common pattern; the conditional logit assumes a common utility function, a limitation the latent class model addresses.

Average relative attribute importance was computed in three steps:

  1. For each attribute, subtract its lowest level estimate from its highest to get a range.
  2. Divide each range by the largest range, giving relative importance per attribute within each class.
  3. Average the values across the two classes.
Primary results: the two-class model

All five attributes were statistically significant in the primary model. Estimated group shares were 46.5% and 53.5%. Both groups showed a general disutility for sharing, with positive rejection intercepts of 0.48 and 0.59 (both p < 0.001).

  • Shared ranking: both groups gave the same importance ranking: new data user, reason, information & consent, review, original collector.
  • Different levels: groups differed on which level they preferred within each attribute, and their orders were exact reverses on collector, recipient and review.
  • Common features: neither group ranked opt-in consent first, and both groups' top review option was some form of review.

Significance of individual levels within each group is reported in Table 4. Latent classes are model-based preference patterns, not observed personality types.

Secondary results: the conditional logit check

Eight of the thirteen estimated levels were significant (pseudo R² 0.111; LR chi² 410.87, p < .001). Compared with the reference levels, and holding other variables constant:

  • Original collector: sharing was less likely when it was a technology company than a healthcare provider; an academic project was not significant (p = 0.970).
  • New data user: no level was significant.
  • Reason: product or service development raised the likelihood of sharing and marketing lowered it, relative to quality evaluation.
  • Information & consent: informed-only and informed-with-opt-out raised it relative to opt-in, and not being informed lowered it.
  • Review: review of transfer only raised it relative to review of transfer and use, and no review lowered it.

Conditional logit coefficients give direction, not the size of differences between levels, so this model corroborates the latent class results without replacing them.

Limitations and what comes next
  • Sample composition: education was uneven (42.1% high school, about 27% master's, other levels around 10% or less). Earlier research links education to data-sharing attitudes, so the preferences may mainly describe these education groups. A more balanced sample could yield more classes or different preferences.
  • Timing: fieldwork ran April to August 2021, during the COVID-19 aftermath. Earlier research suggests the pandemic raised willingness to share, so comparisons across time periods would be useful.
  • No covariates: the latent class model includes none. Age, trust, and privacy and technology attitudes from the survey are natural next additions. One idea is to cluster respondents on trust and privacy attitudes and use those clusters as covariates.
  • Stated choices: the experiment measures decisions in described scenarios, not real transfers of health records.
  • Scope: the findings inform governance considerations; they are not a legal-compliance assessment or proof that a policy would increase sharing.
